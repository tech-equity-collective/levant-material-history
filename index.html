Levant Material History and Archaeogenomic Archive

This independent digital archive compiles peer-reviewed, empirical, and historical data to document the material realities and demographic continuity of the Southern Levant. Operating under rigorous evidentiary standards, this repository prioritizes primary physical and biological data over political and nationalist mythology.

The Archaeogenomics of Continuity: Paleogenomic Evidence of Indigenous Lineage in the Southern Levant

Abstract: Ancient-DNA research provides strong evidence of substantial population continuity in the Levant across millennia. Agranat-Tamir et al. (2020) found that the great majority of present-day Jewish and Arabic-speaking Levantine groups are consistent with having 50% or more of their ancestry from populations related to those who lived in the Bronze Age Levant and Chalcolithic Zagros. Palestinians were among the present-day populations included in that analysis. Haber et al. (2017) separately demonstrated substantial continuity between Bronze Age Canaanite genomes and present-day Lebanese populations. Together, these findings contradict simple demographic-replacement narratives and support substantial regional continuity, while not establishing a single fixed percentage of Bronze Age ancestry for all modern Palestinians. The Islamic conquest of the Levant in the 7th century brought major political, linguistic, religious, and cultural change, but the available genetic evidence does not support treating it as a wholesale population replacement.
Scientific References:
• Haber, M., et al. (2017). "Continuity and Admixture in the Last Five Millennia of Levantine History from Ancient DNA." AJHG, 101(2).
• Agranat-Tamir, L., et al. (2020). "The Genomic History of the Bronze Age Southern Levant." Cell, 181(5).

The 1929 Buraq Uprising: Deconstructing Colonial Imbalances and Land Displacement

In August 1929, widespread violence broke out across Mandatory Palestine. Conventional accounts often reduce the events to a religious dispute around the Western Wall/Buraq or to communal hostility detached from the political conditions of the Mandate. The British-appointed Shaw Commission, however, identified the fundamental cause as Palestinian fear for their economic future through Zionist immigration and land purchase, together with fear of eventual political domination. The Commission also documented land transfers during the 1920s that resulted in Palestinian cultivators being displaced from land on which they had depended for their livelihoods.

The immediate escalation around the Buraq/Western Wall must therefore be understood within that wider colonial political economy. The Commission identified the Zionist demonstration at the Wall on 15 August 1929 as the incident that contributed most to the outbreak, while subsequent violence included killings of both Palestinians and Jews. British forces retained the decisive monopoly of organized coercive power under the Mandate and killed large numbers of Palestinians while suppressing the uprising. Treating the events simply as spontaneous ethnic or religious rioting obscures the underlying struggle over immigration, land, displacement, colonial rule, and political sovereignty.
